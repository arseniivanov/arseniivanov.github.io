# A post-scarcity world (of information)

Over the last years or so, there has been a lot of blogs popping up dealing with the question of taste. [Paul Grahams](https://paulgraham.com/goodtaste.html) from 2021 as well as this anonymous one from [quarter--mile](https://www.quarter--mile.com/Tasty). Both are less than a 5 minute read each.
The blogs are covering a philosophical gate-keeping classic, what is "**good taste**"?

This blog is not primarily intended to answer this question philosophically, but I have seen this seeping into my daily life more and more by the month. As the amount of information created is exploding due to the LLMs, not only due to just raw output of text but also it's aggregates by simplifying creation of frameworks, images, videos, experiments, data insights, businesses, etc. Won't everyone in any informational role reach a point where they simply cannot follow along with whatever they are invested in?

Most likely. And what will you have to do? You will need to choose and filter out the information that you think is valuable to you. It will be up to every person to somehow curate their news, feeds, blogs, whatever in order to choose what information they engage with during the day (despite this problem pitch, i sadly don't have a product to sell you today that solves this, and even if I did, you would have to rely on my bias which might not be meaningful to you, and any such service you can find would most likely have to sell you something or use your data at some point).

What you choose to engage with will both be a creator and a propagator of your **taste**. Choose poorly, and you will be stuck in mediocre low-signal hype-bubbles of some field. Choose well and you will be able to cultivate a taste which will give you an advantage for understanding where things are going for your field and give you a better base for creating value. Since all your peers are wielding the same informational tools as you, choosing what to build and why will need to be your unique advantage.
## Practical taste in the post-scarcity informational world
In the world of fashion, and as a fantastic fashion youtuber [ash callaghan](https://www.youtube.com/@ashleighcallaghan) predicted using her fashion knowledge and later on supported with Google trends -- **authenticity** was a word of early 2026, look at the graph below or go to [google trends](https://trends.google.com/trends/) (albeit the tool is perhaps losing relevance by the day unless it's hooked up to Gemini asap).
![[Pasted image 20260511214659.png]]

I personally think, that this is partially because of the mass availability of information and social media filter bubbles. As the time between a cultural movement/style and it's monetization shrinks due to social media, the only thing left for people is to frantically switch and recycle new microtrends(see 90% of fashion influencers), or to be themselves/be **authentic**(until that in itself becomes a brand, and you start playing the person that you are expected to be based on your audience. [geohot mentions this as a reason in one of his latest blogs on why he does not stream anymore](https://geohot.github.io/blog/jekyll/update/2026/05/03/punk-or-why-i-dont-stream.html)).
In fashion, a similar concept to taste has gone under the name "**the sauce**". You look at someone who has dressed up, and you can often if they have no specific intent with their outfit, if they are pretending and putting on a persona or if they have **the sauce**. 

Of course there is a lot that goes into fashion and there is a lot more to it, such as a LOT of practice, keeping up to date with current trends, having historical knowledge on the area(what goes with what, why) and confidence that is required to produce the feeling of someone having **the sauce**. And funnily if you replace the word fashion above with anything technical, like Neural Networks, or Machine Learning it still makes sense, only differing on terminology.

As we can see, the foundations of taste seem to be multidisciplinary and tied to information and how you wield it.

In some sense, taste could be defined as the learned ability to recognize which qualities matter and then rank plausible alternatives accordingly, especially when those qualities cannot yet be fully formalized/defined.
### Taste as a future signal of job competence
I have both interviewed for, and been interviewing people myself for various technical roles in the last years. In general, tech recruitment has gone through a roller coaster in the last years. 

Let's take a **Research Scientist** in a technical field as an example role.

In the early 2010s, from what I have been told by older colleagues, they barely had any requirements on any research scientist. You were supposed to have a Masters degree, be interested in the field, and sometimes have a good personality as a bonus. In the late 2010s, with globalization the competition heated up. You were now supposed to have all of the above, and good coding skills. If you also have some paper at a conference, that is great.

As we are now in the mid 2020s, we still have all of the above(maybe + a couple of papers), but something disrupting has happened. Research idea generation is essentially free. Coding is essentially free. You can brainstorm 100 ideas with the LLMs in an hour and build prototypes for 5 of them. The ability to do this is now the bottom bar expected for every candidate. So as a recruiter, what can you then look for when you are interviewing? 

I want to make the argument for that **taste** is a logical next candidate here. When information and information creation is abundant, selection of **what** to create using **why** becomes the bottleneck. Very much in line with the [bitter lesson](http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html) which places search as the arbitrarily scale-able feature alongside learning.

That brings the next obvious question, how do you discern taste in someone? 
### Probing taste
We have agreed that there is a massive, as Gleick would say, [flood of information](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/8701960-the-information). Given this fact, we make the assumption that not all information is made equal, and as previously mentioned taste is the ability to select the relevant pieces and engage with those.

So what would you ask in a interview? You could for example have a setup like this:
Present the candidate with 4 ideas with various details and tell them that they can only choose 1. Try to curate the selection in a way where 3 of the ideas are less meaningful, scalable, or lack something from your understanding of the field. You then let the person pick one and explain why they did so. There is one "good" answer, but it might actually be that they manage to convince you otherwise on one of the ideas in a way you find meaningful. In other words, letting the person present their bets, worldview and being attentive to if their answer is  technobabble/virtue signaling or genuine understanding.

Another thing can be selection of relevant signal from noise in your area. It can be that for example in graphics rendering, a hyperrealistic-looking NPC adds less value than a slightly less realistic, but more fluid/natural moving NPC. There is a video example of this from some game from a couple of years ago that I sadly can't find where a character is picking up a cup of coffee from a table. In one shot, the quality of the shot/character/environment is degraded from the original, but the character slightly braces/tenses their arm just before picking up the coffee cup, anticipating the weight of it. This small, almost unnoticeable feature adds to the realistic experience more than the degradation of the image quality does, as the 'roboticness' of the scene is decreased. Someone with taste will need to be able to discern this difference, perhaps not being able to explain in detail why, but by selecting the "better" representation.

The attentive reader can see that we made an assumption here that thing A was "better" than thing B. This is completely dependent on your field, culture, target problem, and group of people you are working with.

In both of these cases, it's interesting for the interviewer to see if the person is able to select the signal among the noise.
### How do I curate what ends up being my taste?

It's good to recognize that taste as an adjective is a feature that is dependent on others. You can never say that you have the taste or have the sauce, it's something that you project and get validated by having other people engage with your ideas. 

I believe that in order to curate taste, you need to make an effort to clean up your informational diet. You need to consciously choose what will appear in your feed and what opinions are worthwhile to take time from your day. Follow people in your field of interest that are sharing interesting material. Read books and blogs of people that have an impact in your field, try to understand why they are choosing their direction, if you are lucky they will tell you.

I personally like to engage with people over blogs, and over 1:1 communication such as mail or in-person talking. I believe that this is the best way to get honest opinions.

I think the late 2020s will be a lot about practicing taste and demonstrating it. It will be requirement as information floods every channel, and many prior traditional signals of validity become invalid. Good luck navigating the flood.
